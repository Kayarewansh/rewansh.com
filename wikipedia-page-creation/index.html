Wikipedia Page Creation

Wikipedia decides who gets an article, not an agency. This service starts with an honest notability assessment, builds the genuine independent press coverage that assessment requires, and guides eligible subjects through Wikipedia's own Articles for Creation review — with any paid contribution disclosed exactly as Wikipedia's policy requires. The Process

Five Steps, In This Order.

Step What Happens
1. Eligibility Review Existing independent coverage is mapped against Wikipedia's notability guidelines before anything else happens. If the bar isn't met yet, you get told that directly.
2. Source Collection & Validation Real, independent secondary sources — journalism, industry publications, verifiable records — are gathered and checked against Wikipedia's reliability standards.
3. Neutral Drafting The article is written in Wikipedia's neutral point of view, cited to the validated sources — not promotional copy repurposed from a website.
4. Disclosed Submission via AfC Submitted through Wikipedia's own Articles for Creation review queue, with any paid involvement disclosed on-wiki. Reviewer decisions typically take 15-30 days.
5. Post-Publication Monitoring 30 days of monitoring after publication for edits, tagging, or deletion discussions — Wikipedia pages are never permanently "done."

Why Submissions Fail

The Reasons Wikipedia Drafts Actually Get Declined.

Most declined drafts fail for one of a small number of predictable reasons — all catchable before submission, not after.

Reason What It Looks Like
Insufficient notability Coverage exists but is local-only, routine business-listing coverage, or a single feature — Wikipedia generally expects multiple, substantial, independent pieces over time, not one write-up.
Sources aren't actually independent Press releases, sponsored or "contributed" articles, interviews where the subject is the only voice, and byline-for-hire placements don't count — even when they run on a recognizable publication's domain.
Promotional tone Language like "leading," "innovative," or "award-winning" without a cited, verifiable source for the claim reads as advertising to reviewers, not neutral encyclopedic writing.
Undisclosed conflict of interest A draft submitted by an undisclosed paid editor, or by the subject themselves without disclosure, is treated as a policy violation independent of whether the content itself is otherwise fine.
Notability that hasn't aged yet A recent funding round or product launch alone rarely clears the bar — reviewers weigh sustained, independent attention over time more heavily than a single news cycle.

Sourcing Standards

What Actually Counts as a Reliable, Independent Source.

This is the single most misunderstood part of the process — and the reason a source review happens before any drafting starts.

Generally Counts

  • ✔ Staff-written features in established national or trade publications
  • ✔ Investigative or analytical journalism that discusses the subject in depth
  • ✔ Academic papers, industry reports, or books that cover the subject substantively
  • ✔ Award citations from independently governed, notable award bodies

Generally Doesn't Count

  • ✘ Press releases and wire-service reprints, even on major domains
  • ✘ Sponsored, "contributed," or paid-placement articles
  • ✘ Interviews or Q&As where the subject is the primary source of every claim
  • ✘ Company blogs, LinkedIn posts, crowdfunding pages, and self-published bios
